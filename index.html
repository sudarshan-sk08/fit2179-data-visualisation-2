Australian Bushfires

Every summer, Australia prepares for fire season. This is a period of intense heat, dry conditions and rapidly spreading flames that threaten lives, homes, and ecosystems across the continent. Such events make global headlines due to the severity despite most other countries also battling wildfires of their own. This visualisation explores the scale, geography, climate drivers, financial cost, and the people on the frontline of Australia's bushfires.

Wildfires Globally

Bushfires, or wildfires as they are more commonly known globally, are events that affect almost every country. While some countries face increased burning per wildfire event occasionally due to an increasing fire risk, the data shows that Australia consistently ranks among the world's most fire-affected nations. This map shows the average area burned per wildfire event by country. The countries in grey don't have sufficient data.

Use the year slider to see how fire severity has shifted globally between 2012 and 2026.

The Scale of Australian Bushfires

Australia has always experienced bushfires, but the frequency and severity of catastrophic seasons has increased markedly in recent decades. It shows that the area burnt is consistently high, and rises occasionally to extreme levels approximately every 10 years. The area burnt by these wildfires can often be greater than the area of many countries.

A key thing to note is that while the 2019-2020 Black Summer was incredibly extreme, this was due to multiple small scale bushfires. This means that a singular bushfire didn't burn as much area, but collectively all bushfires had an immense effect.

Where and When Bushfires Occur

Through satellite data collected by the VIIRS satellite by NASA FIRMS, individual fire hotspot anomalies can be found. The dataset is immense as majority of the detections are reflective of low-intensity grassfires and other small scale controlled burning of land. As a result, the data has been filtered to only show fires that have an average intensity greater than 50 MW. It shows that the north of the country burns frequently but it's primarily comprised of smaller scale fires. In contrast, the south-east of the country burns far less often but each event is capable of devastating entire communities.

The Forest Fire Danger Index (FFDI) measures how dangerous conditions are for fire on any given day. The heatmap below shows average FFDI across Victoria's nine fire regions by month. The Mallee and Wimmera in the north-west consistently record the highest values. The 2019-20 Black Summer stands out across many regions and a wide timeframe.

Link to the Temperature

Temperature extremes are one of the key drivers of severe fire conditions. This can be as a result of vegetation drying out in periods of high temperature, low humidity and no recent rain. This leads to the vegetation becoming more flammable and fires are more likely to start, and continue to burn in such weather conditions.

As a result, it is important to note the maximum temperatures found in each region. However, as temperatures can be varying across the day, it can often be confusing to find what is important.

This chart shows the highest maximum and minimum temperatures recorded in each state each year. The data is collected manually through an interface on the Bureau of Meteorology's website.

Which state runs hottest? This bump chart tracks the annual ranking of each state by its highest recorded maximum temperature. Rank 1 means the hottest state that year. Western Australia and South Australia battle for the top spots most years, while Tasmania consistently anchors the bottom. In general, the years where rankings shuffle dramatically tend to coincide with the worst fire seasons.

However, it is worth noting that there isn't a particularly direct relationship between bushfires and the temperature. While both can be linked with each other, only having a high temperature isn't a sign of bushfire activity.

The Financial Cost

The insured cost of Australia's worst bushfires runs into the billions. While the cost of all bushfires is high and cannot be understated, the severity of the Black Summer is shown clearly. It was found to have generated $2.4 billion in insurance claims alone. This is more than four times the next costliest event and is linked to the vast number of people that were affected.

Values are normalised to 2022 values so events across different years are directly comparable. Data is sourced and filtered from the Insurance Council of Australia.

Not all fires destroy the same things. This chart breaks down insurance claims by category for each major event - domestic building, contents, motor, and commercial property. Community fires like Tathra and Yarloop are almost entirely residential, while Perth Hills and Black Summer show a far higher proportion of commercial losses.

The location and the type of insurance claims also sheds light onto the industry that is primarily active within such regions. This is shown through the Great Ocean Road in 2015 where the primary affected regions involved holiday houses and therefore fell under the domestic building category.

Spotlight on Firefighters

A lot of respect must be paid to the firefighters who risk their lives to control the fires and save communities. This involves domestic firefighters and those who fly into the country from other nations to aid with the firefighting effort. Australia's fire service relies overwhelmingly on volunteers, yet that volunteer base is shrinking and ageing. Understanding the workforce behind bushfire response is essential to understanding Australia's long-term fire readiness.

Each dot represents 500 firefighters. Gold dots are salaried career staff and red dots are volunteers. The sea of red tells the story immediately that volunteers outnumber salaried firefighters by more than 6 to 1. NSW's column towers over every other state, but even there the gold is barely visible at the base. It highlights the efforts from volunteers who take time out of their lives to put their lives at risk, and save communities.

Australia is losing its volunteer firefighters. Between 2015-16 and 2024-25, NSW alone lost nearly 29,000 volunteers which is a decline of 36%. Every state except WA recorded a fall. As the volunteer base shrinks, the pressure on career fire services and on communities in fire-prone areas grows significantly.
The volunteer workforce is also ageing. Nationally, more than 60,000 volunteer firefighters are aged 60 or older and forms the single largest age group, making up 38% of all volunteers. By contrast, salaried firefighters peak in the 30-39 bracket. When today's 60+ volunteers retire, there is no clear pipeline of younger replacements.
